## What the chart shows
A DXA scan measures bone mineral density at the spine and hip. The report plots your result against age. The bands show where it falls compared with a healthy young adult, expressed as a **T-score**:

- **Normal:** −1.0 or higher
- **Low bone mass (osteopenia):** between −1.0 and −2.5
- **Osteoporosis:** −2.5 or lower

Each step of 1.0 is roughly one standard deviation, and each step down roughly doubles fracture risk.

## Which sites count
Both the **spine and the hip** need to be scanned, because they can differ. The diagnosis uses the **lowest** T-score of the lumbar spine (L1–L4), total hip or femoral neck. The forearm (one-third radius) is added when the spine or hip cannot be read reliably, for example after hip replacements or with severe spinal arthritis.

## The T-score is not the whole story
- A **hip or spine fracture from a minor fall** means osteoporosis, whatever the T-score
- With osteopenia, a high **FRAX** 10-year risk (≥20% major fracture or ≥3% hip) is treated like osteoporosis
- Most fractures happen in people in the osteopenia range, simply because there are more of them

## Clinician teaching points
- T-scores apply to postmenopausal women and men ≥50; use **Z-scores** (≤ −2.0 = "below expected range for age") in premenopausal women, men <50 and children
- Exclude vertebrae with fractures or artefact (need ≥2 evaluable); follow-up comparisons need the same machine and the least significant change for the site
- A low Z-score at any age should prompt a [[Secondary causes workup]]
