## What the test is
A quick, non-contrast CT of the heart, with a radiation dose similar to a mammogram. Bright white specks along the coronary arteries are **calcified plaque**. The software adds them up into an Agatston score.

## What it tells you
- Calcium is a **footprint** of plaque: soft, non-calcified plaque does not show up, so the score underestimates total plaque
- The higher the score, the higher the risk of heart attack over the next 10 years, regardless of the cholesterol number
- The score is also reported as a **percentile** for age, sex and race. Being at or above the 75th percentile is a risk enhancer even when the absolute score is modest

## What it changes
| Score | What it usually means for treatment |
|---|---|
| 0 | Low near-term risk; a statin can often be deferred and the scan repeated in 3–7 years. **Not reassuring** with diabetes, current smoking, familial hypercholesterolemia or premature family history |
| 1–99 | Plaque is present; treatment is generally favored, especially from age 55 or at ≥75th percentile |
| 100–299 | Statin recommended |
| ≥300 | High risk; treat intensively |
| ≥1000 | Risk similar to people who have already had an event; LDL-C goal <55 mg/dL |

## Common questions
- **"Will a statin raise my score?"** Statins can increase calcium density as plaque stabilises. A rising score on treatment is not treatment failure, so we do not usually repeat the scan once treatment has started
- **"My score is 0, am I clear?"** Low risk now, not no risk. Soft plaque can be present, especially in younger people and smokers

## Clinician teaching points
- The 2026 guideline folds CAC into a Calculate–Personalize–Reclassify approach alongside PREVENT-ASCVD
- Category cut-points here are the ones in common use (0, 1–99, 100–299, ≥300); some patient handouts use 1–100 / 101–299. Check exact guideline wording on CAC 1–99 and the ≥1000 goal against the full text before relying on them
